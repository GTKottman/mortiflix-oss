
# Picture scoring

The method behind the music step: drama organised in time. (Read it whole before the first blueprint.)

Compose music as drama organized in time. Treat theory, composition, and synchronization as one system: first determine what the scene needs, then design music whose internal logic serves that need.

Do not imitate a copyrighted score or reproduce passages from reference works. Extract abstract principles from references and create new musical material.

## 1. Establish the dramatic job

Before choosing notes, identify:

- whose perspective the audience inhabits;
- the emotional state at the beginning and end;
- what changes during the scene;
- information the music should reveal, conceal, contradict, or reinforce;
- whether silence is stronger than music;
- the cue's required start, end, and important synchronization points.

Summarize the cue in one sentence using a transformation such as: `uncertainty -> recognition -> dread`.

Do not merely label emotions. Identify the dramatic movement between them.

## 2. Spot the picture

Build a chronological spotting map before composing substantial material.

For each meaningful event record:

`time | visual/narrative event | dramatic significance | possible musical response`

Classify important moments as:

- **structural**: scene entrance, transition, climax, reveal, cut, ending;
- **dramatic**: realization, reversal, decision, threat, intimacy, loss;
- **physical**: door slam, impact, gesture, camera move, edit;
- **optional**: events that need not receive explicit musical emphasis.

Do not hit every visible action. Excessive synchronization makes a cue feel mechanical. Favor events that alter meaning.

## 3. Design a musical concept

Choose a small vocabulary before writing the cue:

- tonal center or modal center;
- harmonic character;
- one or two motifs;
- characteristic interval(s);
- rhythmic identity;
- register;
- density;
- articulation;
- timbral identity;
- approximate tempo or tempo range.

Prefer a constrained vocabulary that can transform over a large collection of unrelated ideas.

## 4. Compose piano-first when useful

Use piano as a reduction of the dramatic architecture even if the final cue will be orchestrated.

### Harmony

Control expectation through:

- tonic stability and departure;
- predominant/dominant motion when functional harmony is appropriate;
- modal harmony and pedal tones when functional direction should weaken;
- inversions and voice leading to change intensity without changing chord identity;
- suspensions, appoggiaturas, altered tones, chromatic approach tones, borrowed harmony, and common-tone connections for controlled friction;
- harmonic rhythm: change chords faster to increase activity, slower to suspend time;
- withholding cadences when the scene must remain unresolved.

Do not equate major with happy or minor with sad. Register, spacing, rhythm, articulation, dynamics, harmonic rhythm, orchestration, and context can dominate mode.

### Voice leading

Prefer purposeful motion between individual voices. Retain common tones where useful, move other voices economically, and treat large leaps as expressive events rather than default chord-to-chord behavior.

Check outer voices separately. A convincing bass line and melodic line can make comparatively simple inner harmony feel intentional.

### Melody and motifs

Construct motifs from compact, recognizable properties such as:

- interval contour;
- rhythmic cell;
- repeated note;
- characteristic leap;
- short scale fragment;
- harmonic implication.

Develop rather than continually replace material. Transform motifs through transposition, inversion, augmentation, diminution, fragmentation, rhythmic displacement, reharmonization, register change, altered articulation, or changed accompaniment.

A motif should survive transformation while retaining enough identity for the listener to recognize its dramatic association.

### Phrase and form

Let musical phrases converse with visual phrases, but do not require them to have identical boundaries.

Use antecedent/consequent relationships, continuation, extension, elision, interruption, deceptive resolution, and asymmetrical phrase lengths as dramatic tools.

For picture, a musically "incorrect" phrase length can be correct if the dramatic event demands it.

## 5. Shape tension deliberately

Treat tension as multidimensional. Increase or decrease any combination of:

- harmonic dissonance;
- tonal ambiguity;
- chromaticism;
- register height or depth;
- rhythmic subdivision;
- syncopation;
- dynamic intensity;
- density;
- orchestral weight;
- repetition;
- instability of bass;
- phrase incompleteness;
- proximity to expected resolution.

Do not continuously increase every parameter. Contrast makes intensity legible.

Use expectation strategically. Establish a pattern before violating it.

## 6. Synchronize music to picture

### Hit points

For important hit points, consider four solutions:

1. **Direct hit**: musical event occurs at the visual event.
2. **Anticipation**: music changes shortly before it, preparing perception.
3. **Aftermath**: music reacts afterward, allowing the image to land first.
4. **No hit**: music deliberately ignores the event to preserve larger continuity.

Choose according to dramatic function rather than mathematical neatness.

### Tempo mapping

When several important hits must align naturally, test tempos rather than forcing awkward bars.

For constant tempo:

`seconds per beat = 60 / BPM`

`seconds per bar = beats_per_bar * 60 / BPM`

Use these relationships to find tempos that place major events near useful beat or bar locations.

Minor events may fall between beats. Do not distort the entire cue to synchronize trivial actions.

If necessary, use meter changes, shortened/extended bars, fermatas, tempo ramps, pauses, pickups, or transitional material. Hide technical synchronization inside musical gestures.

## 7. Map dramatic events to musical behavior

Use relationships such as these as starting hypotheses, not formulas:

- uncertainty -> incomplete phrases, ambiguous bass, restrained dynamics;
- recognition -> motif clarification, registral focus, harmonic recontextualization;
- mounting danger -> contraction of rhythmic values, persistent ostinato, rising density;
- grief -> reduced activity, delayed resolution, exposed register, sparse texture;
- intimacy -> smaller register, softer attacks, reduced orchestral distance;
- revelation -> harmonic reinterpretation, arrival, sudden subtraction, or silence;
- unresolved ending -> avoid complete tonic closure or destabilize the final sonority.

Contradiction is allowed. Cheerful music over disturbing imagery, for example, can create irony or emotional distance. State why the contradiction serves the scene.

## 8. Orchestrate after the cue works structurally

Do not use orchestration to conceal weak composition.

When expanding a piano reduction, assign functions rather than simply distributing notes:

- bass foundation;
- harmonic bed;
- pulse/ostinato;
- primary motif;
- counterline;
- texture;
- accent;
- transition gesture.

Consider register and spectral density. Avoid placing every element in the same octave or giving every instrument continuous activity.

Reserve new timbres, registers, and ensemble weight for moments where their arrival has dramatic value.

## 9. Respect dialogue and sound design

Music shares the soundtrack with speech, ambience, and effects.

Under dialogue:

- simplify competing melodic activity;
- avoid unnecessary density in speech-critical registers;
- use rests and sustained material deliberately;
- leave room for important consonants, breaths, and effects;
- avoid musical accents that unintentionally reinterpret dialogue.

When a sound effect already supplies the impact, the score may prepare it or react to it instead of doubling it.

## 10. Revise in layers

Review a cue separately for:

1. dramatic function;
2. synchronization;
3. musical form;
4. harmony and voice leading;
5. motif development;
6. rhythm and pacing;
7. register and density;
8. orchestration/timbre;
9. dialogue and sound-design space;
10. ending and transition into the next scene.

Fix structural problems before polishing voicings or production.

## Output workflow

When asked to score a video or scene, produce as much of the following as the available information permits:

1. **Dramatic reading**: concise interpretation of the scene's trajectory.
2. **Spotting map**: timestamps and significant events.
3. **Musical concept**: tonal center, harmonic language, motifs, tempo, meter, texture, instrumentation.
4. **Cue map**: section-by-section musical plan aligned to timestamps.
5. **Composition**: original chords, melody, bass, rhythms, voicings, MIDI-oriented note data, notation guidance, or DAW instructions as requested.
6. **Synchronization notes**: identify direct hits, anticipations, aftermaths, and intentionally ignored events.
7. **Revision pass**: test whether the cue supports narrative rather than merely matching motion.

If no video is supplied, request or work from a timestamped scene description. If the user supplies only a musical idea, develop the composition first and leave explicit synchronization until picture information exists.

## Quality rules

- Prioritize narrative meaning over constant synchronization.
- Prefer development of limited material over unrelated musical ideas.
- Preserve musical phrasing while accommodating picture.
- Use silence intentionally.
- Make important moments more distinct by keeping surrounding material restrained.
- Explain compositional decisions in terms of their audible or dramatic effect.
- Give concrete notes, chords, rhythms, timestamps, or DAW actions when the user asks for actionable composition rather than theory.
- Keep generated music original. Do not reconstruct recognizable copyrighted melodies, cue passages, or close stylistic replicas of a living artist.
